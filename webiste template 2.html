(a)Overview

Naïve Bayes (NB) is a family of supervised classifiers built on Bayes' theorem. Given a record with features x = (x₁, …, xₙ), it asks: for each possible class C, how probable is that class given what we see? It then predicts the class with the highest probability.

P(C | x) = P(C) · P(x | C) / P(x) Naïve assumption: P(x | C) = P(x₁ | C) · P(x₂ | C) · … · P(xₙ | C) Prediction: ŷ = argmax_C [ log P(C) + Σᵢ log P(xᵢ | C) ]

The "naïve" part is the assumption that every feature is conditionally independent of the others once the class is known. That is rarely true. A card's rarity and its subtype (VMAX, ex) clearly move together. Even so, NB works well in practice because it only needs the correct class to come out on top, not perfectly calibrated probabilities. The model trains in one pass by counting, needs little data, scales to thousands of features, and its learned probabilities are easy to read.

P(C) is the prior: how common each class is in the training data. P(xᵢ | C) is the likelihood of a feature within a class. Working in log space avoids multiplying many tiny probabilities into numeric underflow.

What NB is used for

  • Text classification: spam filtering, sentiment analysis, topic and news tagging, where documents become word-count vectors.
  • Fast baselines for any categorical or count-based classification problem before trying heavier models.
  • Recommendation and filtering (for example, "will this user like this item").
  • Medical and risk screening from symptom or attribute checklists.
  • Here: price-tier prediction for trading cards from their attributes.

Multinomial vs. Bernoulli Naïve Bayes

The variants differ in how they model P(xᵢ | C), and so in what kind of data they accept.

Multinomial NB (used here)Bernoulli NB
Feature typeNon-negative counts or frequencies (how many times a feature occurs)Binary: present (1) or absent (0)
LikelihoodP(xᵢ|C) = (N_Ci + α) / (N_C + α·n)P(x|C) = Πᵢ pᵢᶜ^xᵢ · (1 − pᵢᶜ)^(1−xᵢ)
Absent featuresIgnored: a 0 count contributes nothingPenalized: the absence of a feature that is common in a class counts as evidence against it
Typical useWord counts in long documents; count-encoded attributesShort texts, yes/no checklists, word presence

In the Multinomial formula, N_Ci is the total count of feature i in class C, N_C is the total of all feature counts in C, and n is the number of features. In the Bernoulli formula, pᵢᶜ is the fraction of class-C records in which feature i is present.

Laplace smoothing (α). If a feature never appears with a class in training, its raw likelihood is 0, and that single zero wipes out the whole product. Adding α (here α = 1) to every count keeps every probability above zero. Both models in this tab use α = 1.

(b)Data Prep

Supervised learning needs labeled data. Each record must carry the correct answer (the label) so the model can learn which feature patterns go with which class. Rows without a label cannot be used to train or to test, because there is nothing to learn from or check against.

1. Source data and the label

The source is the March 2025 snapshot of the modern (2019–2025, Sword & Shield and Scarlet & Violet) and vintage (1999–2002, Base through Legendary Collection) card files: 7,213 cards in total. Each card has up to three TCGplayer market prices (Normal, Reverse Holofoil, Holofoil). I took the highest of those as the card's Market Price and binned it into the label Price Tier:

Price TierRuleCardsShare
Lowunder $1 (bulk)4,16962.3%
Mid$1 to under $101,76726.4%
High$10 and up (chase cards)75511.3%

522 cards had no market price at all. They have no label, so they were dropped before modeling. That leaves 6,691 labeled cards.

2. Converting every feature to non-negative counts

Python's sklearn.naive_bayes.MultinomialNB accepts only numeric, non-negative values, which it treats as counts. Mixed text and numbers have to be converted first:

  • One-hot (0/1) columns for Era, Supertype, energy Type, Rarity, and release year.
  • Multi-hot columns for Subtypes, since one card can be several at once ("Basic, V" sets both sub_Basic and sub_V).
  • Binned one-hot columns for HP and for the largest attack damage. Raw HP values like 330 would otherwise outweigh every 0/1 feature in a count-based model.
  • Small integer counts for number of attacks and retreat cost (0 to 5).

Name, ID, artist, image links, and the price columns were left out. ID and name are identifiers, not attributes. The price columns are where the label came from, so including them would leak the answer into the features.

3. Splitting into a Training Set and a Testing Set

The prepared data was split 70% / 30% with train_test_split(test_size=0.30, stratify=y, random_state=42):

  • Training Set (4,683 cards) is the only data the model sees while learning its priors and likelihoods.
  • Testing Set (2,008 cards) is held back and used once, to measure accuracy on cards the model has never seen.
  • Stratified sampling keeps the Low / Mid / High mix the same in both sets, which matters here because High cards are only about 11% of the data.
  • A fixed random_state makes the split reproducible.

Why the sets must be disjoint. Every card goes to exactly one set; the code checks that the two sets share zero rows. If a card were in both, the model would be graded on a question it had already seen the answer to. Accuracy would be inflated and would no longer tell us how the model handles new cards, which is the whole point of testing. A disjoint Testing Set stands in for future, unseen data.

"""
Naive Bayes on Pokemon TCG cards: predict a card's PRICE TIER from its attributes.

Label (supervised target): Price Tier, from the March 2025 TCGplayer market price
(highest of the Normal / Reverse Holofoil / Holofoil market prices):
    Low  : < $1
    Mid  : $1 to < $10
    High : >= $10

Features: card attributes turned into non-negative COUNTS, as sklearn's
MultinomialNB requires (one-hot indicators + small integer counts).

Usage:
    python nb_pokemon.py <modern_mar2025.csv> <vintage_mar2025.csv> [out_dir]
"""
import sys, os, json
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.colors import LinearSegmentedColormap
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB, BernoulliNB
from sklearn.metrics import (accuracy_score, confusion_matrix,
                             classification_report, precision_recall_fscore_support)

MODERN, VINTAGE = sys.argv[1], sys.argv[2]
OUT = sys.argv[3] if len(sys.argv) > 3 else "nb_output"
os.makedirs(OUT, exist_ok=True)
SEED = 42
TIERS = ["Low", "Mid", "High"]

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 1. load + label
mod = pd.read_csv(MODERN);  mod["Era"] = "Modern"
vin = pd.read_csv(VINTAGE); vin["Era"] = "Vintage"
cards = pd.concat([mod, vin], ignore_index=True)

price_cols = ["TCG Market Price USD (Normal)",
              "TCG Market Price USD (Reverse Holofoil)",
              "TCG Market Price USD (Holofoil)"]
cards["Market Price"] = cards[price_cols].max(axis=1)

# Only LABELED rows can be used for supervised learning: drop cards with no price
labeled = cards.dropna(subset=["Market Price"]).copy()
labeled["Price Tier"] = pd.cut(labeled["Market Price"], [0, 1, 10, np.inf],
                               right=False, labels=TIERS).astype(str)
print(f"All cards: {len(cards)}   labeled (have a price): {len(labeled)}")
print(labeled["Price Tier"].value_counts())

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 2. features -> counts
def n_attacks(s):
    return 0 if pd.isna(s) or s == "" else len(str(s).split("), "))

def max_damage(s):
    if pd.isna(s):
        return 0
    nums = [int("".join(ch for ch in part if ch.isdigit()) or 0)
            for part in str(s).split("(")[1:]]
    return max(nums) if nums else 0

X = pd.DataFrame(index=labeled.index)
# one-hot categorical attributes (0/1 counts)
for col, prefix in [("Era", "era"), ("Supertype", "super"), ("Types", "type"),
                    ("Rarity", "rarity")]:
    X = X.join(pd.get_dummies(labeled[col].fillna("None"), prefix=prefix).astype(int))
# subtypes can hold several tokens ("Basic, V") -> multi-hot
sub = labeled["Subtypes"].fillna("None").str.get_dummies(sep=", ").add_prefix("sub_")
X = X.join(sub)
# release year (set age) one-hot
X = X.join(pd.get_dummies(labeled["Release Date"].str[:4], prefix="year").astype(int))
# HP bins one-hot (raw HP would swamp the other counts in a multinomial model)
hp_bin = pd.cut(labeled["HP"].fillna(0), [-1, 0, 60, 100, 150, 220, 1000],
                labels=["none", "1-60", "61-100", "101-150", "151-220", "221+"])
X = X.join(pd.get_dummies(hp_bin, prefix="hp").astype(int))
# damage bins one-hot
dmg = labeled["Attacks"].apply(max_damage)
dmg_bin = pd.cut(dmg, [-1, 0, 50, 120, 200, 10000],
                 labels=["0", "1-50", "51-120", "121-200", "201+"])
X = X.join(pd.get_dummies(dmg_bin, prefix="dmg").astype(int))
# small integer counts
X["n_attacks"] = labeled["Attacks"].apply(n_attacks)
X["retreat_cost"] = labeled["Retreat Cost"].fillna(0).astype(int)
X.columns = [c.replace(" ", "_") for c in X.columns]
y = labeled["Price Tier"]
assert (X.values >= 0).all(), "MultinomialNB needs non-negative features"
print("Feature matrix:", X.shape)

# save the raw labeled sample and the prepared numeric sample
raw_cols = ["ID", "Name", "Era", "Supertype", "Subtypes", "HP", "Types", "Attacks",
            "Retreat Cost", "Set Name", "Release Date", "Rarity", "Market Price",
            "Price Tier"]
labeled[raw_cols].to_csv(f"{OUT}/labeled_cards_raw.csv", index=False)
prepared = X.copy(); prepared.insert(0, "Price Tier", y); prepared.insert(0, "ID", labeled["ID"])
prepared.to_csv(f"{OUT}/prepared_numeric_data.csv", index=False)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 3. train / test split
ids = labeled["ID"]
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test, id_train, id_test = train_test_split(
    X, y, ids, test_size=0.30, random_state=SEED, stratify=y)
assert set(X_train.index).isdisjoint(X_test.index), "train and test must be disjoint"
train_df = X_train.copy(); train_df.insert(0, "Price Tier", y_train); train_df.insert(0, "ID", id_train)
test_df = X_test.copy();   test_df.insert(0, "Price Tier", y_test);   test_df.insert(0, "ID", id_test)
train_df.to_csv(f"{OUT}/train_set.csv", index=False)
test_df.to_csv(f"{OUT}/test_set.csv", index=False)
print(f"Train: {len(X_train)}  Test: {len(X_test)}  overlap: "
      f"{len(set(X_train.index) & set(X_test.index))}")

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 4. models
mnb = MultinomialNB(alpha=1.0).fit(X_train, y_train)
pred = mnb.predict(X_test)
acc = accuracy_score(y_test, pred)
cm = confusion_matrix(y_test, pred, labels=TIERS)
print("\nMultinomial NB accuracy:", round(acc, 4))
print(classification_report(y_test, pred, labels=TIERS, digits=3))

bnb = BernoulliNB(alpha=1.0, binarize=0.5).fit(X_train, y_train)
bpred = bnb.predict(X_test)
bacc = accuracy_score(y_test, bpred)
bcm = confusion_matrix(y_test, bpred, labels=TIERS)
print("Bernoulli NB accuracy:", round(bacc, 4))

baseline = (y_test == y_train.value_counts().idxmax()).mean()
print("Majority-class baseline:", round(baseline, 4))

# accuracy by era
era_test = labeled.loc[X_test.index, "Era"]
era_acc = {e: float(accuracy_score(y_test[era_test == e], pred[(era_test == e).values]))
           for e in ["Modern", "Vintage"]}

# most indicative features per class: log P(f|class) - mean log P(f|other classes)
flp = pd.DataFrame(mnb.feature_log_prob_, index=mnb.classes_, columns=X.columns)
top = {}
for c in TIERS:
    others = [o for o in TIERS if o != c]
    diff = flp.loc[c] - flp.loc[others].mean()
    top[c] = diff.sort_values(ascending=False).head(8).round(3).to_dict()

p, r, f, s = precision_recall_fscore_support(y_test, pred, labels=TIERS)
metrics = dict(accuracy=acc, bernoulli_accuracy=bacc, baseline=baseline,
               n_cards=len(cards), n_labeled=len(labeled), n_features=X.shape[1],
               n_train=len(X_train), n_test=len(X_test),
               class_counts=y.value_counts().reindex(TIERS).to_dict(),
               train_counts=y_train.value_counts().reindex(TIERS).to_dict(),
               test_counts=y_test.value_counts().reindex(TIERS).to_dict(),
               confusion_matrix=cm.tolist(), bernoulli_confusion_matrix=bcm.tolist(),
               per_class={t: dict(precision=float(p[i]), recall=float(r[i]),
                                  f1=float(f[i]), support=int(s[i]))
                          for i, t in enumerate(TIERS)},
               era_accuracy=era_acc, top_features=top,
               priors=dict(zip(mnb.classes_, np.exp(mnb.class_log_prior_).round(4))))
with open(f"{OUT}/metrics.json", "w") as fh:
    json.dump(metrics, fh, indent=2, default=float)

    fig_w = max(8, 1.45 * df.shape[1]); fig_h = 0.3 * (len(df) + 2)

sample_raw = pd.concat([
    labeled[labeled.Era == "Modern"][raw_cols].sample(7, random_state=SEED),

table_png(sample_raw, f"{OUT}/img_raw_sample.png", "Labeled raw data (10 random cards)")

show_cols = ["ID", "Price Tier", "era_Vintage", "super_Pokémon", "type_Fire",
             "rarity_Common", "rarity_Rare_Holo", "sub_Basic", "sub_V", "hp_61-100",
             "n_attacks", "retreat_cost"]

table_png(prepared.loc[sample_raw.index[:8], show_cols], f"{OUT}/img_prepared_sample.png",

# class balance in train vs test
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 2.2))

for t in TIERS:

(d)Results

On the 2,008 held-out test cards, Multinomial NB scored 80.4% accuracy. Always guessing the most common tier (Low) would score 62.3%, so the model adds about 18 percentage points over that baseline. Bernoulli NB, trained on the same features reduced to present/absent, scored 75.4%.

Confusion matrix

Rows are each card's actual tier; columns are what the model predicted. The diagonal holds the correct predictions. Shading follows the percentage of each row, so the smaller High tier reads as clearly as Low.

  • Low cards are easy. 87% of bulk cards were caught, with 91% precision. Commons and uncommons are almost always under $1.
  • Mid is the catch-all. 76% recall, but it also absorbs mistakes from both sides: some Low cards look like Mid (holo rares, V cards), and many High cards are predicted Mid.
  • High cards are the hard case. Only 52% were recognized. Two-fifths were labeled Mid. When the model does say High, it is right 70% of the time.
  • Errors are almost always one tier off. Only 26 test cards were confused between Low and High.

What the model learned

Because NB stores a probability for every feature in every class, we can read it directly. The chart ranks features by how much more likely they are within a tier than in the other two (difference in log P(feature | tier)).

Era matters. Accuracy was 81.7% on modern test cards but 61.1% on vintage ones. Vintage prices depend on condition, nostalgia, and specific Pokémon (a Base Set Charizard against a Base Set Ponyta), and those things are not in the printed attributes. Vintage cards are also only about 6% of the training data.

(e)Conclusions

A card's printed attributes alone predict its price tier correctly about 4 times in 5. That is well above guessing, from a model that trains in under a second. For a collector, a seller sorting a box, or anyone looking at the hobby's economics, this says several things:

  • Rarity is the main driver of value. The rarity symbol carried most of the signal. Common and Uncommon cards are reliably bulk, which supports the usual practice of sorting by rarity first.
  • Modern premium printings are what sell. Special Illustration Rare, Illustration Rare, Hyper Rare, and rainbow or shiny printings mark the cards most likely to reach $10 or more. Ordinary holos and V cards mostly land in the $1–$10 range.
  • Set age is a value signal. Release year was among the strongest High-tier features. Older vintage sets (especially Legendary Collection, 2002) carry a premium regardless of the card's game stats.
  • Game stats barely matter for price. HP, damage, retreat cost, and energy type ranked far below rarity and era. Collectors pay for scarcity and artwork, not playability.
  • The model can't spot the chase cards inside a rarity. About half of High-tier cards were underpriced as Mid. What separates a $15 card from a $3 card of the same rarity is usually the specific Pokémon (Charizard, Umbreon, Pikachu), the artwork, and demand. Those are not among these features. Adding the Pokémon's name or popularity, or the artist, would likely help most for the High tier and for vintage cards.

In practical terms, the model can be used as a fast first-pass sorter. A card it calls Low can be put in bulk with confidence. A card it calls Mid or High is worth looking up individually.

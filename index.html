How to find the card with the most potential in prices increase?

Introduction

Pika, pika, pika.

  • Overview of the Pokémon Card Market Pokémon began in 1996 as a Nintendo video game franchise centered around collecting and battling Pokémon. Its popularity quickly expanded to the trading card game, turning Pokémon cards into a global collecting phenomenon. What began primarily as a children's hobby has developed into a substantial secondary market, where rare, vintage, and professionally graded cards are traded by collectors and investors. Over the past decade, that market has exploded rather than simply grown, with some cards appreciating by over 3000 percent. This growth has attracted increasing attention from investors and researchers interested in understanding the factors that drive card prices and market trends. The market has also developed many features similar to traditional financial markets, including price volatility, speculation, professional grading, and increasingly sophisticated pricing data. In early 2026, this trend reached a new milestone when a rare Pikachu Illustrator card reportedly sold for $16.49 million at auction, setting a record for the most expensive Pokémon card ever sold. The combination of rapidly increasing prices, growing participation from investors, and increasingly available market data makes the Pokémon card market an interesting setting for studying price trends and potential investment strategies.
  • Common Investment Knowledge Experienced collectors generally consider several factors when evaluating whether a Pokémon card is likely to retain or increase its value. 1)**Rarity and print run** are important, as early sets, limited promotional releases, and cards with low population counts among graded copies can command significant premiums. 2)**Condition and grade** are also critical. For example, a PSA 10 ``Gem Mint'' copy can be worth many times more than a worn copy of the same card because collectors place a high value on pristine condition. 3)**Character popularity** is another major factor. Iconic Pokémon such as Charizard, Pikachu, and Mewtwo often command higher prices than equally rare cards featuring less popular Pokémon because demand is influenced by nostalgia and brand recognition as well as scarcity. 4)**Print era and historical significance** can also contribute to value. For example, first-edition and shadowless cards from the original Base Set are highly sought after because of their historical importance. Cards associated with printing errors, promotional events, or notable artwork can also carry a premium because of their unique history. For investment purposes, collectors often favor cards that combine several of these characteristics. A card supported by multiple sources of demand may be less dependent on any single factor and may therefore be more resilient to changes in market trends.
  • Card Grading Business Card grading serves as a quality-control and authentication process that transforms a raw trading card into a standardized and more easily tradeable asset. PSA (Professional Sports Authenticator) is one of the most widely recognized grading companies for Pokémon cards. When a card is submitted, PSA graders evaluate the alignment of the image within the borders, the sharpness and condition of the corners and edges, and surface defects such as scratches, print lines, or indentations. PSA then assigns a numerical grade from 1 to 10, with a PSA 10 ``Gem Mint'' representing a virtually flawless card. The grade can have a substantial effect on a card's market value because it provides a standardized assessment of condition. A sealed, tamper-evident PSA case with a verified grade and certification number allows buyers to evaluate a card without having to inspect it in person. This standardization is particularly important for high-value cards, which can be traded online for thousands or even tens of thousands of dollars. The grading system has also created a market strategy commonly known as "card flipping". Investors search through bulk collections, local card shops, garage sales, and online listings for raw cards that appear undervalued, then submit them for professional grading. If a card receives a high grade, particularly a PSA 9 or 10, its market value can increase substantially relative to its raw price. However, a lower-than-expected grade can reduce or even eliminate the potential profit after accounting for the purchase price and grading fees.
  • Beyond the Grade: Predicting the Market Card flipping may appear to be a probability game on the surface, but investors who consistently profit from it are ultimately engaging in a market-prediction strategy rather than simply gambling on grading outcomes. Chasing cards that are already popular is often a less reliable path to profit. When a card is widely recognized as valuable, many investors compete for the same copies, and sellers can raise raw-card prices until the potential profit margin after grading becomes small. The greater opportunity may come from identifying cards before they become widely sought after. A card may gain popularity because of an upcoming set release, increased attention to a particular Pokémon in the games or anime, a competitive deck that drives demand for certain cards, or a mismatch between the card's current price, rarity, and graded population. Identifying these opportunities requires more than the ability to recognize cards with perfect margins. It requires an ongoing understanding of market trends, an understanding of which cards are likely to attract collectors, and a systematic research process for identifying cards that may currently be overlooked. In this sense, successful investors are not simply reacting to cards that are already popular. They are attempting to anticipate which cards are likely to become popular in the future and position themselves before the broader market recognizes their value.

    Overview

    Partitioning a dataset into different categories is a common way to gathering information. The problem of automatically identifying meaningful groupings in dataset is called clustering. Meaningful groupings can bring interpretation and optimal decisions for each group. In machine learning, this grouping idea is called "clustering". Because the categories in clustering are automatically discovered partitions of an unlabeled dataset, this process is also called unsupervised learnig. Every clustering method needs two choices. The first is a distance measure tha how close two data points are. The second is a rule for turning those distances into groups. Because features with large ranges would dominate any distance, all features here are standardized to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 before clustering. In the following, a couple of common clustering will be discussed, and they will be applied to the Pokémon dataset to produce interpretable results.

    • K-means represents each cluster by its centroid. A point belongs to the cluster whose centroid is nearest. The centroid is the mean of the points assigned to it. uses Euclidean distance, which is the straight-line distance between two cards in feature space. $$d_{E}(x, y) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - y_i)^2}$$ It is sensitive to magnitude: a card with very high HP and price is far from a card with low values, even if their profiles have the same overall pattern. K-means pick the number of clusters, k, using the following two ways:
      1. Elbow. Plot WCSS against k. It always falls as k grows, but the drop flattens once the real groups are covered. The bend (the "elbow") is a sensible k.
      2. Silhouette. For a data point $i$, let $a_i$ be the average distance from point $i$ to all other points in the same cluster, and let $b_i$ be the average distance from point $i$ o all points in the nearest neighboring cluster. The Silhouette score is $$s_i = \frac{b_i - a_i}{\max (a_i, b_i)} \in [-1,1].$$ $s_i$ close to +1 indicates a strong match, around 0 sits on a border, close to -1 is likely is the wrong cluster. The optimal number of clusters (k) is the k with highest average Silhouette score.
    • Hierarchical Clustering is another unsupervised technique that groups data into a clusters based on similarity. It builds a tree‑like structure (dendrogram) that helps visualize relationships and decide the optimal number of clusters. It shows how individual data points or groups of data merge together. The bottom shows each data point as its own group and as we move up, similar groups are combined. Height is the distance at which two clusters merged. Low joins are tight groups; tall joins bridge groups that are far apart. Cut the tree with a horizontal line. The number of vertical strands it crosses is the number of clusters. The cut in the below figure sits in the tall gap and gives two clusters: {A,B} and {C,D,E}. The hierarchical clustering uses cosine distance. It ignores length and compares direction only: two cards are close if their features are high and low in the same places, regardless of how extreme they are. $$d_{\cos}(x, y) = 1 - \frac{x \cdot y}{\lVert x \rVert \, \lVert y \rVert}$$
    Using clustering for discovery. The plan is to use clusters as a map of the card market, then look for cards that do not behave like their group.
    1. Define market segments. Name each cluster by its profile and compare price behavior across segments. This shows whether value is driven by gameplay stats, age, or rarity.
    2. Find mispriced cards. Within a cluster, cards with similar stats should have similar prices. A card priced well above or below its cluster's median is a candidate for being over- or undervalued.
    3. Spot momentum. Feb-to-Mar price change is a clustering feature. Cards whose change differs sharply from their cluster's mean change are worth tracking as the next monthly snapshot arrives.

    Overview

    PCA takes a dataset with many possibly correlated variables and map it in a eigenspace. The new axes, called principal components, are chosen so that: 1) The first principal component points in the direction where the data varies the most; 2) all principal components are orthogonal to each other; 3) the components are uncorrelated with one another. Usually, the first two principal components take account most of the variance, so PCA on a two-dimensional version of the data that keeps most of its structure.

    The PCA follows the following procedure: 1) standardize the data; 2) Compute the covariance matrix Σ; 3) find the eigenvectors and eigenvalues; 4) sort the eigenvectors by eigenvalue, largest first; 5) choose the top k eigenvectors; 6) project the data.

    Eigenvalues and eigenvectors are the heart of PCA. For a square matrix A, an eigenvector v is a nonzero vector whose direction does not change when A is applied to it. It only gets stretched or shrunk: $$\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}$$ The scalar λ is the corresponding eigenvalue. It tells you how much v is stretched. When A is the covariance matrix Σ, eigenvectors and eigenvalues have a direct statistical meaning. Eigenvector is a direction in feature space, i.e. a principal component. Its entries (“loadings”) show how much each original feature contributes to it. Eigenvalue is the variance of the data along that direction, i.e. how much information that component carries. Therefore, the top eigenvector is the direction of maximum variance. Dimensionality reduction means transforming data from a high-dimensional space (many features) into a lower-dimensional space while keeping as much meaningful structure as possible. Humans can only see 2 or 3 dimensions. Projecting a 50-feature dataset onto PC1 and PC2 gives a scatter plot where clusters, outliers, and trends often become visible right away. Low-variance components often capture mostly measurement noise. Dropping them and reconstructing the data acts as a denoising filter. Storing k components instead of d features reduces storage. This is the idea behind “eigenfaces” in image compression and recognition.

    The reason of using the dimensionality reduction method, such as PCA, is the curse of dimensionality, which describes how many things go wrong as the number of features grows.

  • Data becomes sparse. With a fixed number of samples, high-dimensional space is mostly empty, so every point is effectively isolated.
  • Distances lose their meaning. In high dimensions, the distance to a point’s nearest neighbor and to its farthest neighbor become nearly equal. Methods built on distance, such as k-means, struggle because “close” and “far” stop being distinguishable.
  • Overfitting. When the number of features approaches or exceeds the number of samples , a model can fit the training data perfectly by memorizing noise. It then generalizes poorly. Each extra dimension also requires much more data to estimate parameters reliably.
  • Computational cost. Training time and memory use all grow with dimensionality.
  • Noise accumulation. Every irrelevant feature adds a little noise. Across hundreds or thousands of features, that noise can drown out the real signal.
    • In practice, PCA is usually computed with Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of the centered data matrix. It gives the same result and is more numerically stable. Every matrix, no matter its shape, can be written as a rotation, then a set of independent stretches, then another rotation. That statement is the singular value decomposition, $A = U \Sigma V^T$. PCA is not a different machine, it is the SVD applied to mean-subtracted data.

    Conclusions

    The card data has two main dimensions, gameplay power and collectible value, and they are nearly independent. Month-to-month price change sits outside both, so the PCA confirms it can’t be explained by the card features we have. Two components capture 50% of the information in the seven variables. The features are only loosely correlated, so PCA can summarize them but not collapse them into one or two numbers without losing a lot. What this means for next steps:

    1. Collector value depends on age (and rarity), not gameplay. Any price model needs rarity and era as inputs, not just stats.
    2. Drop one of HP or max damage before clustering again, or cluster on the first 3–5 PC scores. Otherwise power is double-counted.
    3. Price movement needs its own analysis with more monthly snapshots. The current features can’t explain it.

    Overview

    Naïve Bayes (NB) is a family of supervised classifiers built on Bayes' theorem. Given a record with features $x = (x_1, x_2, ..., x_n)$, it asks: for each possible class $C$, how probable is that class given what we see? It then predicts the class with the highest probability. $$P(C | x) = P(C) · P(x | C) / P(x)$$ $$\text{Naive assumption:} \quad P(x | C) = P(x_1 | C) · P(x_2 | C) \cdot … \cdot P(x_n | C)$$ $$\text{Prediction:} \quad \hat{y} = argmax_C [ \log P(C) + Σ_i \log P(x_i | C) ]$$ The "naive" part is the assumption that every feature is conditionally independent of the others once the class is known. That is rarely true. A card's rarity and its subtype (VMAX, ex) clearly move together. Even so, NB works well in practice because it only needs the correct class to come out on top, not perfectly calibrated probabilities. The model trains in one pass by counting, needs little data, scales to thousands of features, and its learned probabilities are easy to read.

    $P(C)$ is the prior: how common each class is in the training data. $P(x_i | C)$ is the likelihood of a feature within a class. Working in log space avoids multiplying many tiny probabilities into numeric underflow.

    NB can be used for the following applications:

    • Text classification: spam filtering, sentiment analysis, topic and news tagging, where documents become word-count vectors.
    • Fast baselines for any categorical or count-based classification problem before trying heavier models.
    • Recommendation and filtering (for example, "will this user like this item").
    • Medical and risk screening from symptom or attribute checklists.
    • Here: price-tier prediction for trading cards from their attributes.

    Multinomial vs. Bernoulli Naïve Bayes

    The variants differ in how they model P(xᵢ | C), and so in what kind of data they accept.

    Multinomial NB Bernoulli NB
    Feature type Non-negative counts or frequencies Binary: yes (1) or no (0)
    Likelihood $P(x_i|C) = (N_{C_i} + \alpha) / (N_C + \alpha \cdot n)$ $P(x|C) = \prod_i p_i^{x_i} \cdot (1-p_i)^{1-x_i}$
    Typical use Word counts in long documents, counted-encoded attributes Yes/no checklist, word presence

    In the Multinomial formula, $N_{C_i}$ is the total count of feature $i$ in class $C$, $N_C$ is the total of all feature counts in $C$, and $n$ is the number of features. In the Bernoulli formula, $p_i$ is the fraction of class-$C$ records in which feature $i$ is present.

    Laplace smoothing (α). If a feature never appears with a class in training, its raw likelihood is 0, and that single zero wipes out the whole product. Adding $\alpha$ (here $\alpha = 1$) to every count keeps every probability above zero. Both models in this tab use α = 1.

    Conclusions

    A card's printed attributes alone predict its price tier correctly about 4 times in 5. That is well above guessing, from a model that trains in under a second. For a collector, a seller sorting a box, or anyone looking at the hobby's economics, this says several things:

    • Rarity is the main driver of value. The rarity symbol carried most of the signal. Common and Uncommon cards are reliably bulk, which supports the usual practice of sorting by rarity first.
    • Modern premium printings are what sell. Special Illustration Rare, Illustration Rare, Hyper Rare, and rainbow or shiny printings mark the cards most likely to reach $\$10$ or more. Ordinary holos and V cards mostly land in the $\$1–\$10$ range.
    • Set age is a value signal. Release year was among the strongest High-tier features. Older vintage sets (especially Legendary Collection, 2002) carry a premium regardless of the card's game stats.
    • Game stats barely matter for price. HP, damage, retreat cost, and energy type ranked far below rarity and era. Collectors pay for scarcity and artwork, not playability.
    • The model can't spot the chase cards inside a rarity. About half of High-tier cards were underpriced as Mid. What separates a $15 card from a $3 card of the same rarity is usually the specific Pokémon (Charizard, Umbreon, Pikachu), the artwork, and demand. Those are not among these features. Adding the Pokémon's name or popularity, or the artist, would likely help most for the High tier and for vintage cards.

    In practical terms, the model can be used as a fast first-pass sorter. A card it calls Low can be put in bulk with confidence. A card it calls Mid or High is worth looking up individually.

    Overview

    A decision tree is a supervised model that predicts a label by asking a sequence of yes/no questions about the features. Each internal node tests one feature against a threshold ("is the rarity tier above 4?"), each branch is an answer, and each leaf holds a prediction: the majority class of the training rows that ended up there (for classification).

    Trees are used for classification (spam or not, which price tier), regression (predicting a price directly), and as the building block of stronger ensembles such as Random Forests and gradient-boosted trees. Their biggest strength is that they are readable. Anyone can follow a path from the root to a leaf and see exactly why a card was labeled "High". They also handle mixed feature types, need no scaling, and capture interactions automatically, since a question asked lower in the tree only applies to the cards that reached that branch.

    Anatomy of a decision tree, using questions the real model in section (d) actually learned. A card enters at the root and follows one path down to a leaf.

    What a tree does geometrically. Fit on only two features (rarity tier and card age), a depth-3 tree carves the space into axis-aligned rectangles and gives each one a label. Old holo rares (rarity 3, >5 years) become a High region; modern ultra and secret rares (4–6) land in Mid, where High and Mid cards are badly mixed. That mixing is what impurity measures.

    Gini, Entropy and Information Gain.To grow a tree, the algorithm has to decide which question to ask at each node. It tries every feature and every candidate threshold and keeps the split whose children are the most "pure", meaning each child is dominated by one class. Gini impurity and entropy are two ways to put a number on impurity; information gain measures how much a split reduces it. The follwoing are the formulas, where $p_i = $ share of class $i$ in a node.

    $$\text{Gini(node) } = 1 − \sum_i p_i^2, \qquad 0 = \text{pure}, \quad 0.667 \text{ max for 3 classes}$$ $$\text{Entropy(node)} = − \sum_i p_i · \log_2 p_i, \qquad 0 = \text{pure}, \quad 1.585 \text{max for 3 classes}$$ $$\text{Gain(split) } = \text{Impurity(parent) } − \sum_k (n_k / n) \cdot \text{Impurity(child_k)}$$

    Both measures are zero when a node holds a single class and largest when the classes are evenly mixed. Gini is slightly cheaper to compute (no logarithm) and is scikit-learn's default; entropy comes from information theory and penalizes mixed nodes a little more sharply. In practice they usually pick the same or very similar splits. The weighted average in the gain formula matters: a split that creates one tiny pure child and one huge mixed child should not score well, so each child counts in proportion to its size.

    Worked example: which question splits ten cards better? Take ten cards: 5 are High and 5 are Low. Compare two candidate questions.

    Node Cards High / Low Gini Entropy
    Parent (all ten) 10 5 / 5 $1-(0.5^2 + 0.5^2) = 0.500$ 1.000
    Split A: "Is the card vintage?"
    Vintage 4 3 / 1 $1-(0.75^2 + 0.25^2) = 0.375$ 0.811
    Modern 6 2 / 4 $1-(0.333^2 + 0.667^2) = 0.444$ 0.918
    Split B: "Is rarity Ultra Rare or higher?"
    Ultra higher 4 4 / 0 $0.000$ 0.000

    Information gain with entropy:

    • Split A: $1.000 − (0.4 × 0.811 + 0.6 × 0.918) = 0.124$
    • Split B: $1.000 − (0.4 × 0.000 + 0.6 × 0.650) = 0.610$

    Information gain with Gini:

    • Split A: $0.500 − (0.4 × 0.375 + 0.6 × 0.444) = 0.083$
    • Split B: $0.500 − (0.4 × 0.000 + 0.6 × 0.278) = 0.333$

    Split B wins under both measures, because it isolates a pure group of four High cards. The tree would ask the rarity question first.

    Why there are infinitely many possible trees

    • Continuous thresholds. A numeric feature like card age can be cut at any real number. Any cut between two neighbouring training values (say 13.80 and 13.99 years) sorts the training data the same way, but each is a different tree that will treat new cards differently.
    • Stopping choices. Maximum depth, minimum samples per leaf, minimum gain to split, and pruning strength can all vary, and each setting produces a different tree. Without limits, a tree can keep splitting until every leaf is pure.
    • Criterion and features. Gini, entropy or log-loss; which features are allowed; how categorical values are encoded.
    • The data itself. A different random training sample, or a slightly different label threshold, changes the best split at the root, and every split below it changes too. Trees are known for this instability.

    Conclusion

    Decision trees can sort Pokémon cards into Low, Mid and High price tiers with about 86% accuracy from printed information alone, well above the 63% you would get by guessing "under a dollar" for everything. The trees also make it clear why a card is priced the way it is.

    • How a card is printed matters more than how it plays. Whether a card comes in both normal and reverse-holo versions was the most important feature in every tree. That one fact separates bulk commons and uncommons (95% under $1) from the holo-only rares and chase cards. HP, attack damage and retreat cost added almost nothing. Collectors price scarcity and art, not battle strength.
    • Age is a shortcut to High. Every multi-printing vintage card (1999–2002) the tree saw was worth more than $10, and 46% of all priced vintage cards are High compared with 9% of modern ones. An old Base Set uncommon can outprice a brand-new ultra rare.
    • Modern High cards are the hard part. Among modern ultra and secret rares, High and Mid overlap heavily. The tree needs extra cues to tell them apart: Special Illustration and Hyper rares, recently released sets, and popular Pokémon. A popular early-generation Pokémon in a late "secret" set slot is the clearest High signal the no-rarity tree found.
    • Rarity can be inferred. With rarity removed, set position and Pokémon popularity recovered almost the same accuracy (80.6% vs 81.1%). The card number alone reveals much of what the rarity symbol says.
    • Bigger trees are not better. The unlimited tree memorized its training data (99.8%) yet did no better on new cards than a 39-leaf tree. Limiting depth and leaf size gave a simpler model that generalizes just as well.

    For a collector or seller, this means a short checklist predicts most of a card's value: Is it holo-only? Is it vintage? Is it a Special Illustration, Hyper or Secret rare? Is it a fan-favorite Pokémon? Cards that pass several of those checks are the ones to sleeve, grade, or price-check before selling. Cards that fail all of them can usually go in the bulk box.

    Overview

    Regression is a family of supervised models that learn how a set of input features relates to a target. Both models in this tab start from the same idea, a weighted sum of the features, but they answer different questions. Linear regression predicts a number. Logistic regression predicts a class.

    • Linear Regression predicts a continuous numeric value. It assumes the target is a straight-line combination of the features: $$\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x_1 + b_2 x_2 + \cdots + b_k x_k$$ Each coefficient $b_j$ is the expected change in the target when feature $x_j$ goes up by one unit and the other features stay the same. The coefficients are chosen by ordinary least squares, which picks the line that makes the sum of squared errors $\sum (y - \hat{y})^2$ as small as possible. In this tab, linear regression predicts the market price of a card on a log scale.
    • Logistic Regression is a classifier, despite the name. It predicts the probability that a record belongs to class 1, then turns that probability into a yes/no label. In this tab, it predicts whether a card is valuable (1) or not (0).

    Linear regression fits a straight line to a numeric target (left). Logistic regression fits an S-shaped curve to a 0/1 target, so its output is always a probability between 0 and 1 (right). The data in this figure is for illustration only.

    From a linear score to a probability: the sigmoid. Logistic regression first computes a linear score, exactly like linear regression: $$z = b_0 + b_1 x_1 + \cdots + b_k x_k$$ The score $z$ can be any number from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$, but a probability must lie between 0 and 1. The sigmoid (logistic) function squeezes the score into that range: $$p = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$ Large positive scores give probabilities near 1, large negative scores give probabilities near 0, and $z = 0$ gives exactly 0.5. Read backwards, the score is the log-odds, $z = \ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)$. So logistic regression is a linear model of the log-odds, which is why each coefficient can be read as a change in the odds.

    From a probability to a class. To make a decision, the probability is compared with a threshold, usually 0.5: $$\hat{y} = \begin{cases} 1 \text{ (valuable)} & \text{if } p \geq 0.5 \\ 0 \text{ (not valuable)} & \text{if } p < 0.5 \end{cases}$$ Because $\sigma(0) = 0.5$, this is the same as asking whether $z \geq 0$. A worked example from the fitted model: take a 2-year-old card with 200 HP, retreat cost 2, two attacks, and a top attack of 180 damage. At rarity tier 4 (for example, a V or ex card) the score is $z = -0.97$, so $p = 0.28$ and the card is predicted not valuable. The same card at tier 5 (an Illustration or Secret Rare) gets $z = +0.74$ and $p = 0.68$, so it is predicted valuable. The threshold can be moved: lowering it catches more valuable cards, at the cost of more false alarms.

    Linear Regression Logistic Regression
    Task Regression (predict a quantity) Classification (predict a class)
    Target Continuous number Two classes, coded 0/1
    Output Any real number Probability in (0, 1), then a label

Going from a Common (tier 0) to a chase rare (tier 5) raises the predicted price by a factor of about 144, for an otherwise identical card. Rarity is by far the strongest driver of price.
    • Card_Age_Years ($b = 0.056$). Each extra year since release raises the predicted price by about 14% ($10^{0.056} \approx 1.136$). Older cards go out of print and get scarcer, so they cost more, even after accounting for rarity.
    • Retreat_Cost ($b = -0.064$) is a smaller effect: each extra energy to retreat lowers the price by about 14%. HP and Max_Damage have tiny coefficients because they are counted in single points (+100 HP is only about ×1.30).

    Logistic regression. On the same 1,117 test cards, logistic regression scored 92.1% accuracy, (928 + 101) / 1117. Always guessing "not valuable" would score 84.2%, so the model adds about 8 percentage points over that baseline.

    What one coefficient means for the odds. The coefficient of Rarity_Tier is $b = 1.71$. Each step up the rarity scale adds 1.71 to the log-odds that a card is valuable, so it multiplies the odds by $e^{1.71} \approx 5.5$, holding the other features fixed. For example, if a tier-3 card has odds of 1 to 9 of being worth \$5 or more ($p = 0.10$), the same card at tier 4 has odds of about 5.5 to 9 ($p \approx 0.38$). Likewise, Card_Age_Years has an odds ratio of $e^{0.188} \approx 1.21$: each year of age raises the odds of being valuable by about 21%.

    Conclusions

    A card's printed attributes explain about three quarters of the variation in its (log) price, and they flag cards worth \$5 or more correctly about 9 times in 10. For a collector or investor, this says several things:

    • Rarity is the price. Each step up the rarity ladder multiplies the expected price by about 2.7 and the odds of a card being worth \$5 or more by about 5.5. This agrees with the Naive Bayes and Decision Tree tabs, and puts a number on it.
    • Age adds value. Even with rarity held fixed, older cards cost about 14% more per year. Sets go out of print, the supply of clean copies shrinks, and prices drift up.
    • Game stats barely matter for price. HP, attack damage and number of attacks have tiny coefficients once rarity is known. Collectors pay for scarcity and artwork, not playability.
    • The models can't spot the chase cards. Prices can be estimated to within a factor of about 2.3, but the true chase cards (a Charizard, a popular Illustration Rare) are the ones the linear model underpredicts and the logistic model misses. Their prices depend on which Pokémon it is and how much people like the art. Adding Pokémon popularity or set position, which helped the Decision Tree tab, would be the natural next step.
    • Correlated features favor logistic regression. For the yes/no question "is this card worth \$5?", logistic regression beat Naive Bayes on accuracy, precision, F1 and AUC, because it learns the weights of correlated features together.

    In practical terms, the logistic model can be used as a high-precision filter. A card it calls valuable is almost always worth pulling out and price-checking. The linear model gives a rough price estimate, and the gap between that estimate and the real market price is a starting point for finding cards the market may be over- or under-valuing.

    Overview

    Boosting is an ensemble method: it builds one strong classifier out of many weak ones. Unlike a single decision tree, a boosted model is a team of small trees that vote together. What makes boosting different from other ensembles is that the trees are built one after another, and each new tree concentrates on the cards that the earlier trees got wrong.

    • Bagging (bootstrap aggregating, the idea behind Random Forests) trains every tree independently and in parallel, each on its own random resample of the training data. The trees are usually deep, and every tree gets an equal vote. Averaging many noisy but unbiased trees mainly reduces variance.
    • Boosting trains trees in sequence. Every tree sees the same rows, but the rows are re-weighted after each round so that the cards misclassified so far count for more. The trees are usually very simple, and each tree's vote is weighted by how accurate it was. Adding up many small corrections mainly reduces bias.

    Bagging trains trees in parallel on bootstrap samples and takes an equal-weight vote (left). AdaBoost trains stumps one after another: the rows each stump gets wrong are up-weighted for the next round, and the final prediction is a vote weighted by each stump's $\alpha$ (right).

    Decision stumps: the weak learners. A decision stump is a decision tree with depth 1: one split and two leaves. It asks a single yes/no question, such as "Is the rarity Illustration Rare?" or "Is HP $\leq 185$?", and predicts the weighted majority class on each side. On its own a stump is a weak learner, only somewhat better than guessing. AdaBoost only needs each stump to beat chance on the current weighting of the data, i.e. a weighted error $\varepsilon < 0.5$.

    How AdaBoost reweights the rows. The labels are coded as $y_i \in \{-1, +1\}$. Every one of the $N$ training rows starts with the same weight $w_i = 1/N$. Then, for each round $t = 1, \dots, T$:

    1. Fit a stump $h_t$ using the current row weights. The stump picks the single split with the lowest weighted error.
    2. Measure its weighted error. $\varepsilon_t$ is the total weight of the rows it gets wrong (the weights sum to 1): $$\varepsilon_t = \sum_{i:\, h_t(x_i) \neq y_i} w_i$$
    3. Give the stump a say, $\alpha_t$ (see below). Low error gives a big $\alpha_t$; error near 0.5 gives $\alpha_t$ near 0.
    4. Reweight the rows. Misclassified rows are multiplied by $e^{\alpha_t}$ (they get heavier), correctly classified rows are multiplied by $e^{-\alpha_t}$ (they get lighter), and the weights are renormalized to sum to 1: $$w_i \leftarrow \frac{w_i \, e^{-\alpha_t \, y_i \, h_t(x_i)}}{\sum_j w_j \, e^{-\alpha_t \, y_j \, h_t(x_j)}}$$ Since $y_i h_t(x_i) = +1$ when the stump is right and $-1$ when it is wrong, the next stump is pushed toward the "hard" rows.

    What the weight $\alpha$ of each stump means. $\alpha_t$ is the weight of stump $t$'s vote, i.e. how much the final model trusts it. It depends only on the stump's weighted error: $$\alpha_t = \frac{1}{2} \ln\left(\frac{1 - \varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t}\right)$$ An accurate stump ($\varepsilon = 0.10$) gets $\alpha \approx 1.10$, a stump barely better than chance ($\varepsilon = 0.40$) gets $\alpha \approx 0.20$, and a coin flip ($\varepsilon = 0.5$) gets $\alpha = 0$, so it has no say at all. Above 0.5, $\alpha$ would be negative, meaning "do the opposite of what this stump says". The same $\alpha_t$ also sets how hard the row weights move, so a confident stump changes the data a lot for the next round and a weak one barely changes it.

    A note on scikit-learn: AdaBoostClassifier uses the SAMME algorithm, where $\alpha_t = \eta \cdot \ln\left((1 - \varepsilon_t)/\varepsilon_t\right)$ and $\eta$ is the learning_rate. For two classes this is the formula above scaled by $2\eta$, so it ranks the stumps the same way and gives the same predictions when $\eta = 1$. A smaller learning rate shrinks every $\alpha$, so each round moves the model less and more rounds are needed.

    The final weighted vote. To classify a new card, every stump votes $+1$ or $-1$, each vote is multiplied by that stump's $\alpha$, and the votes are added up. The sign of the total is the prediction: $$H(x) = \text{sign}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T} \alpha_t \, h_t(x)\right)$$ For example, if three stumps vote Yes ($+1$), No ($-1$), Yes ($+1$) with $\alpha = 1.75, 0.50, 0.67$, the score is $1.75 - 0.50 + 0.67 = +1.92 > 0$, so the prediction is Yes. In the card model below, the very first stump asked "Is the rarity Illustration Rare?" and received $\alpha = 1.75$, the loudest single vote in the whole ensemble.

    Data Prep

    Supervised learning needs labeled data. Each record must carry the correct answer (the label) so the model can learn which feature patterns go with which class, and the labeled data is split into a Training Set (to build the model) and a Testing Set (to test it).

    The February 2025 snapshot (modern and vintage, 7213 cards together) is used, so each card appears only once. The label is built from the market price:

    • One price per card. Best Market Price is the highest market price among a card's printings (Normal, Reverse Holofoil, Holofoil).
    • Label. High Value = Yes if the best market price is $\geq \$5$, otherwise No. Just like the Regression tab, \$5 is roughly where a single card is worth pulling out of bulk.
    • Rows dropped. 522 cards had no market price at all, so they have no label and were dropped. That leaves 6691 labeled cards, and 17.2% of them are High Value, so the classes are imbalanced.
    • No leakage. The price columns are used only to build the label. None of them is given to the model.

    Turning categories into numbers. AdaBoost in scikit-learn (and the stumps inside it) only accepts numeric inputs, so the text columns have to be converted first. Mapping categories to integers (Fire = 1, Water = 2, ...) would invent an order that doesn't exist, so one-hot encoding is used instead: every category gets its own 0/1 column.     Next is to split the dataset into a training set and testing set with train_test_split(test_size=0.20, random_state=42, stratify=y).

    • Training Set (80%, 5352 cards) is the only data the model sees while choosing its stumps and their weights. All tuning (cross-validation and the validation curve in Results) also uses only the training set.
    • Testing Set (20%, 1339 cards) is held back and used once, at the very end, to score the model on cards it has never seen.
    • Stratified sampling keeps the share of High Value cards the same in both sets (about 17.2%).
    • Disjoint. The two sets must not share any card. If a card were in both, the model could simply remember its label, and the test score would measure memory instead of how well it works on new cards. An ensemble of hundreds of stumps can memorize a lot, so this matters even more here. The code checks that no row and no card ID appears in both sets before fitting.

    Conclusions

    Using only what a collector can see on the card, AdaBoost labeled about 9 cards in 10 correctly as worth \$5 or more or not. It caught 62% of the valuable cards, and when it called a card valuable it was right 83% of the time. For a collector or investor, this says several things:

    • Rarity is the strongest signal, and the modern chase rarities are near-sure bets. The very first stump asked "Is it an Illustration Rare?" and earned the biggest vote in the model. 96% of Special Illustration Rares, 86% of Illustration Rares, 80% of Hyper Rares and 75% of Rainbow Rares are worth \$5 or more, compared with only 2–3% of Commons and Uncommons. In the modern era, a card's value is largely decided by the rarity tier it is printed at.
    • Era adds value on its own. About half of the priced vintage cards (1999–2002) are worth \$5 or more, compared with 15% of modern cards. Stumps on "Era = Vintage" and "Release Year $\leq$ 2010" were among the earliest and strongest, and AdaBoost got 97% of the held-out vintage cards right. This agrees with the Decision Trees and Regression tabs.
    • Game stats barely matter for price. HP appeared once among the first stumps, but rarity and era carried almost all of the total vote. Collectors pay for scarcity and artwork, not playability.
    • The model can't spot the chase cards inside a rarity. Most errors are valuable cards that look ordinary: a competitively played Uncommon, a Rare of a fan-favorite Pokémon, a vintage card valued for nostalgia. Pokémon popularity, tournament play and condition are not in these features. Adding Pokémon popularity or set position, which helped the Decision Tree tab, is the natural next step to catch the missed 38%.
    • Boosting matched the tree, but did not beat it. 800 one-question stumps matched a tuned depth-10 tree on accuracy and were a little better at finding the valuable minority, while the tree is far easier to read. On this data the features carry most of the signal, so which features the model gets matters more than which model is used.

    In practical terms, AdaBoost can be used as a first-pass sorter that leans toward catching valuable cards. A card it calls High Value is worth pulling out and price-checking, and lowering its decision threshold would catch even more of them at the cost of more false alarms.

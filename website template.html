Supervised learning · Decision Trees

Can a tree tell a $0.20 card from a $20 one?

Using TCGplayer market prices for 7,213 Pokémon cards (modern Sword & Shield / Scarlet & Violet era and vintage 1999–2002 sets), I trained decision trees to sort each card into a price tier from its printed attributes alone.

aOverview

A decision tree is a supervised model that predicts a label by asking a sequence of yes/no questions about the features. Each internal node tests one feature against a threshold ("is the rarity tier above 4?"), each branch is an answer, and each leaf holds a prediction: the majority class of the training rows that ended up there (for classification) or their average (for regression trees).

Trees are used for classification (spam or not, which price tier), regression (predicting a price directly), and as the building block of stronger ensembles such as Random Forests and gradient-boosted trees. Their biggest strength is that they are readable. Anyone can follow a path from the root to a leaf and see exactly why a card was labeled "High". They also handle mixed feature types, need no scaling, and capture interactions automatically, since a question asked lower in the tree only applies to the cards that reached that branch.

Gini, Entropy and Information Gain

To grow a tree, the algorithm has to decide which question to ask at each node. It tries every feature and every candidate threshold and keeps the split whose children are the most "pure", meaning each child is dominated by one class. Gini impurity and entropy are two ways to put a number on impurity; information gain measures how much a split reduces it.

Formulas (pi = share of class i in a node)

Gini(node) = 1 − Σ pᵢ² 0 = pure, 0.667 max for 3 classes Entropy(node) = − Σ pᵢ · log₂ pᵢ 0 = pure, 1.585 max for 3 classes Gain(split) = Impurity(parent) − Σ (nₖ / n) · Impurity(childₖ)

Both measures are zero when a node holds a single class and largest when the classes are evenly mixed. Gini is slightly cheaper to compute (no logarithm) and is scikit-learn's default; entropy comes from information theory and penalizes mixed nodes a little more sharply. In practice they usually pick the same or very similar splits. The weighted average in the gain formula matters: a split that creates one tiny pure child and one huge mixed child should not score well, so each child counts in proportion to its size.

Worked example: which question splits ten cards better?

Take ten cards: 5 are High and 5 are Low. Compare two candidate questions.

Information gain with entropy

Split A: 1.000 − (0.4 × 0.811 + 0.6 × 0.918) = 1.000 − 0.876 = 0.124 Split B: 1.000 − (0.4 × 0.000 + 0.6 × 0.650) = 1.000 − 0.390 = 0.610

Same comparison with Gini

Split A: 0.500 − (0.4 × 0.375 + 0.6 × 0.444) = 0.500 − 0.417 = 0.083 Split B: 0.500 − (0.4 × 0.000 + 0.6 × 0.278) = 0.500 − 0.167 = 0.333

Split B wins under both measures, because it isolates a pure group of four High cards. The tree would ask the rarity question first.

The same arithmetic on the real training set: the root of Tree 1 splits 5,018 cards on n_printings ≤ 1.5. The parent (3,151 Low / 1,312 Mid / 555 High) has Gini 0.525. The “multiple printings” child (2,806 cards, 95% Low) has Gini 0.099 and the “single printing” child (2,212 cards) has Gini 0.588. Weighted child Gini is 0.315, so the Gini gain is 0.210. Measured with entropy instead, the information gain is 0.457 bits (1.279 → 0.822). No other feature or threshold scored higher, which is why it sits at the root.

Why there are infinitely many possible trees

  • Continuous thresholds. A numeric feature like card age can be cut at any real number. Any cut between two neighbouring training values (say 13.80 and 13.99 years) sorts the training data the same way, but each is a different tree that will treat new cards differently.
  • Stopping choices. Maximum depth, minimum samples per leaf, minimum gain to split, and pruning strength can all vary, and each setting produces a different tree. Without limits, a tree can keep splitting until every leaf is pure.
  • Criterion and features. Gini, entropy or log-loss; which features are allowed; how categorical values are encoded.
  • The data itself. A different random training sample, or a slightly different label threshold, changes the best split at the root, and every split below it changes too. Trees are known for this instability.

Because the space of trees is effectively unlimited, algorithms like CART do not search for the single best tree (that problem is NP-hard). They grow one greedily, taking the best split at each node, and use hyperparameters and a held-out test set to choose among the results. Section (d) shows four of these trees built from the same data.

bData Prep

Decision trees are a supervised method. The model learns from examples where the answer is already known, so every training row needs a label. Here the label is the card's price tier, created from its February 2025 TCGplayer market price. A card with no market price has no label and cannot be used to train or test, so those rows were removed.

Supervised models also need two separate sets of labeled rows: a training set the tree learns its splits from, and a testing set it has never seen, used only to measure accuracy.

1. The raw data

2. Labeling and cleaning

  • Combined the Feb 2025 modern (6,345 rows) and vintage (868 rows) files into 7,213 cards, adding an Era column.

  • Label = Low ≤ $1 Mid $1–$10 High > $10. Counts: 4,201 Low, 1,750 Mid, 740 High.

3. Training and testing sets

I used train_test_split(test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=42). It shuffles the 6,691 labeled cards and puts each one into exactly one set: 5,018 training cards (75%) and 1,673 testing cards (25%). Stratifying keeps the Low/Mid/High mix identical in both sets, which matters because only 11% of cards are High. The script then checks that no card ID appears in both sets.

The sets must be disjoint. A tree that is scored on cards it trained on is being asked about answers it has already memorized, so the score says nothing about new cards. Section (d) shows this directly: the unpruned Tree 4 is 99.8% accurate on its own training data but 86.2% on the test set. Only the test score is an honest estimate of how the model will do on cards it has never seen.

Tree 1 · Gini, depth 3

The smallest tree is the easiest to read. Its first question is whether a card is sold in more than one printing. Commons and uncommons almost always come in normal and reverse-holo versions, and 95% of those are Low. Among those multi-printing cards, anything older than 13.9 years (the vintage sets) goes straight to a pure High leaf of 77 cards. On the single-printing side it uses rarity and age, but every one of those leaves predicts Mid. That makes Tree 1 cautious: it never confuses Low with High, yet it catches only 29 of the 185 High test cards.

Tree 2 · Entropy, depth 6 (best)

Allowing three more levels lets the tree separate modern chase cards from ordinary ultra rares. Below the first split it uses rarity tier (Special Illustration, Hyper and Secret rares vs. regular ultras), how recently the card came out, the Pokémon's Dex number and popularity, and the vintage flag. High recall rises from 16% to 65% while overall accuracy reaches 86.5%, and training and test accuracy are almost identical, so this tree is not overfitting.

Tree 3 · Gini, depth 4, without rarity

This tree tests what the model can learn when the official rarity is hidden. It recovers most of the signal from set_position: cards numbered past about 69% of their set's highest number are the full-art and secret slots. It then uses Pokémon popularity and Dex number to pick out the High cards: a frequently printed Pokémon from the first three generations (Dex number ≤ 292) in a late set slot is predicted High. Accuracy is 80.6%, only half a point below Tree 1, which had rarity available.

Confusion matrices

Rows are the true tier and columns the predicted tier, so the diagonal counts correct predictions. In all three trees, almost every mistake is between neighbouring tiers. Tree 2 sends only 7 High cards to Low and no Low cards to High. Most of its errors sit near the $1 and $10 cut-offs, where a few cents or dollars change the label.

Out-of-time check

As a final test, Tree 2 (trained on February prices) predicted the March 2025 tier of the same 1,673 test cards. It scored 87.0%. Only 1.7% of those cards changed tier between the two months, so a month of price movement barely affects the model.

eConclusions

Decision trees can sort Pokémon cards into Low, Mid and High price tiers with about 86% accuracy from printed information alone, well above the 63% you would get by guessing "under a dollar" for everything. The trees also make it clear why a card is priced the way it is.

How a card is printed matters more than how it plays.Whether a card comes in both normal and reverse-holo versions was the most important feature in every tree. That one fact separates bulk commons and uncommons (95% under $1) from the holo-only rares and chase cards. HP, attack damage and retreat cost added almost nothing. Collectors price scarcity and art, not battle strength.
Age is a shortcut to High.Every multi-printing vintage card (1999–2002) the tree saw was worth more than $10, and 46% of all priced vintage cards are High compared with 9% of modern ones. An old Base Set uncommon can outprice a brand-new ultra rare.
Modern High cards are the hard part.Among modern ultra and secret rares, High and Mid overlap heavily. The tree needs extra cues to tell them apart: Special Illustration and Hyper rares, recently released sets, and popular Pokémon. A popular early-generation Pokémon in a late "secret" set slot is the clearest High signal the no-rarity tree found.
Rarity can be inferred.With rarity removed, set position and Pokémon popularity recovered almost the same accuracy (80.6% vs 81.1%). The card number alone reveals much of what the rarity symbol says.
Bigger trees are not better.The unlimited tree memorized its training data (99.8%) yet did no better on new cards than a 39-leaf tree. Limiting depth and leaf size gave a simpler model that generalizes just as well.

For a collector or seller, this means a short checklist predicts most of a card's value: Is it holo-only? Is it vintage? Is it a Special Illustration, Hyper or Secret rare? Is it a fan-favorite Pokémon? Cards that pass several of those checks are the ones to sleeve, grade, or price-check before selling. Cards that fail all of them can usually go in the bulk box.

Limitations: prices are from two snapshots (Feb and Mar 2025), condition and grading are not included, 522 cards with no market price could not be labeled, and the $1/$10 tier boundaries are my own choice. A regression tree on raw price or a Random Forest would be natural next steps.

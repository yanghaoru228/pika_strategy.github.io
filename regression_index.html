Regression

Can a card’s printed attributes predict its market price (linear regression), or whether it is worth $5 or more (logistic regression)?

(a) Overview(b) Data Prep(c) Code(d) Results(e) Conclusions

aOverview

Regression is a family of supervised learning methods that learn a relationship between input features (here, things printed on a card such as HP, rarity, and release date) and a target we want to predict. The two methods on this page share the same core idea, a weighted sum of the features, but they answer different kinds of questions.

Linear regression: predicting a number

Linear regression predicts a continuous numeric value. It assumes the target is roughly a straight-line (linear) combination of the features:

ŷ = b₀ + b₁x₁ + b₂x₂ + … + bₖxₖ

Each coefficient bⱼ is the expected change in the target when feature xⱼ goes up by one unit and the other features stay the same. The coefficients are chosen by ordinary least squares, which picks the line that makes the sum of squared errors (actual − predicted)² as small as possible. For this project, linear regression predicts a card’s market price (on a log scale, explained in Data Prep).

Logistic regression: predicting a class

Logistic regression is used for classification. It predicts the probability that an item belongs to class 1, and then turns that probability into a yes/no label. Here it predicts whether a card is “valuable” (market price ≥ $5) or not. Despite the name, it is a classifier, not a method for predicting numbers.

From a linear score to a probability: the sigmoid

Logistic regression starts exactly like linear regression. It computes a linear score

z = b₀ + b₁x₁ + … + bₖxₖ

The problem is that z can be any number from −∞ to +∞, while a probability must lie between 0 and 1. The sigmoid (logistic) function fixes that:

p = σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z)

Large positive scores give probabilities near 1, large negative scores give probabilities near 0, and z = 0 gives exactly 0.5. Read backwards, z is the log-odds: z = ln(p / (1 − p)). So logistic regression is a linear model of the log-odds. That is why each coefficient has an “odds” interpretation (see Results).

From a probability to a class

To make a decision, the probability is compared with a threshold, usually 0.5:

predict 1 (valuable) if p ≥ 0.5, otherwise predict 0 (not valuable)

Because σ(0) = 0.5, this is the same as asking whether z ≥ 0. Here is a worked example from the fitted model. Take a 2-year-old card with 200 HP, retreat cost 2, two attacks, and a top attack of 180 damage. At rarity tier 4 (for example, an ex or V card) the model’s score is z = −0.97, so p = 0.28, and it is predicted not valuable. The same card at tier 5 (an illustration or secret rare) gets z = +0.74 and p = 0.68, so it is predicted valuable. The threshold can be moved: lowering it catches more valuable cards at the cost of more false alarms.

bData Prep

Both models are supervised: they learn from labeled data, meaning rows where the correct answer (the price, or the valuable/not label) is already known. They also need every feature to be numeric with no missing values. The raw card data does not meet these requirements yet, so it was prepared as follows.

Starting data

I used the February 2025 snapshot of the cleaned card data from earlier tabs (modern_pkmn_cards_feb2025.csv and vintage_pkmn_cards_feb2025.csv, together 7,213 cards with TCGplayer prices dated 2025-02-22). The March 2025 snapshot was not used here, so that each card appears only once.

Steps

  1. Kept Pokémon cards only. Trainer and Energy cards have no HP or attacks, so the features below do not exist for them.
  2. One market price per card. I used the Holofoil market price if the card has one, otherwise the Normal price, otherwise the Reverse Holofoil price. Cards with no price at all were dropped.
  3. Turned text into numbers.
    • Num_Attacks and Max_Damage were parsed from the Attacks text (for example, “Sweet Scent (), Sting (20)” gives 2 attacks and a max damage of 20).
    • Card_Age_Years = years from the release date to the price date.
    • Rarity_Tier maps the 23 rarity names to an ordered scale: 0 Common, 1 Uncommon, 2 Rare/Promo, 3 Rare Holo, 4 ultra-type rares (V, VMAX, ex/Double Rare, Ultra Rare, Shiny, Radiant…), 5 chase rares (Illustration, Special Illustration, Secret, Rainbow, Hyper).
    • HP and Retreat_Cost were already numeric.
  4. Built the two targets (labels).
    • For linear regression, the numeric target is Log10_Price = log₁₀(market price). Prices run from $0.02 to over $1,000 and are heavily right-skewed. On a log scale, a straight line fits much better, and each coefficient becomes a percentage change instead of a dollar change.
    • For logistic regression, the two-class label is Valuable = 1 if the market price is at least $5, otherwise 0. That is roughly the point where a single card is worth pulling out of bulk and selling or protecting on its own. 15.8% of cards are valuable, so the classes are imbalanced.

The result is 5,584 labeled cards (5,270 modern, 314 vintage) with 6 numeric features and 2 labels.

Training set and testing set

The labeled data was split once into a Training Set (80%), used to fit the models, and a Testing Set (20%), used only to score them. I used scikit-learn’s train_test_split with test_size=0.20, random_state=42 for a repeatable shuffle, and stratify=Valuable so both sets keep the same share of valuable cards (training 706 of 4,467, testing 176 of 1,117, both about 15.8%). The same split is reused by linear regression, logistic regression, and Naive Bayes, so their scores can be compared fairly.

Why the sets must be disjoint. The testing set stands in for cards the model has never seen. If a card were in both sets, the model could simply remember its price, and the test score would overstate how well it works on new cards (this is called data leakage). The code checks that no card ID appears in both sets before fitting anything.

cCode

Everything is in one Python script that uses pandas for data prep, scikit-learn’s LinearRegression, LogisticRegression, and GaussianNB for the models, and matplotlib for the figures. It reads the two February CSVs, writes the prepared data, the training set, and the testing set, fits all three models on the same split, and saves every figure and metric shown on this page to results.json.

def max_damage(attacks):
    """Largest printed damage number in the Attacks text, e.g. 'Absorb (10)' -> 10."""
    if not isinstance(attacks, str):
        return 0
    nums = [int(n) for n in re.findall(r"\((\d+)", attacks)]
    return max(nums) if nums else 0


def num_attacks(attacks):
    if not isinstance(attacks, str):
        return 0
    return len(re.findall(r"\(", attacks))


def load_and_prepare(data_dir):
    modern = pd.read_csv(os.path.join(data_dir, "modern_pkmn_cards_feb2025.csv"))
    vintage = pd.read_csv(os.path.join(data_dir, "vintage_pkmn_cards_feb2025.csv"))
    modern["Era"] = "Modern"
    vintage["Era"] = "Vintage"
    df = pd.concat([modern, vintage], ignore_index=True)
    raw_rows = len(df)

    # Pokemon cards only: Trainer/Energy cards have no HP or attacks
    df = df[df["Supertype"] == "Pokémon"].copy()

    # One market price per card: holofoil if the card has one, else normal,
    # else reverse holofoil (the version collectors usually mean)
    df["Market_Price"] = (df["TCG Market Price USD (Holofoil)"]
                          .fillna(df["TCG Market Price USD (Normal)"])
                          .fillna(df["TCG Market Price USD (Reverse Holofoil)"]))

    df["Rarity_Tier"] = df["Rarity"].map(RARITY_TIER)
    df["Retreat_Cost"] = df["Retreat Cost"]
    df["Num_Attacks"] = df["Attacks"].apply(num_attacks)
    df["Max_Damage"] = df["Attacks"].apply(max_damage)
    rel = pd.to_datetime(df["Release Date"], format="%Y/%m/%d")
    priced = pd.to_datetime(df["TCG Price Date"], format="%Y/%m/%d")
    df["Card_Age_Years"] = ((priced - rel).dt.days / 365.25).round(2)

    df = df.dropna(subset=["Market_Price", "HP", "Rarity_Tier", "Card_Age_Years"])
    df = df[df["Market_Price"] > 0]

    # Targets
    df["Log10_Price"] = np.log10(df["Market_Price"]).round(4)          # linear
    df["Valuable"] = (df["Market_Price"] >= VALUE_THRESHOLD).astype(int)  # logistic

    keep = ["ID", "Name", "Set Name", "Era", "Rarity", "Market_Price"] + FEATURES \
        + ["Log10_Price", "Valuable"]
    df = df[keep].reset_index(drop=True)
    df["HP"] = df["HP"].astype(int)
    df["Rarity_Tier"] = df["Rarity_Tier"].astype(int)
    return df, raw_rows

def table_image(frame, path, title, max_rows=8, col_width=None):
    shown = frame.head(max_rows).copy()
    for c in shown.columns:
        if shown[c].dtype.kind == "f":
            shown[c] = shown[c].map(lambda v: f"{v:.2f}")
        shown[c] = shown[c].astype(str).str.slice(0, 22)
    ncol = len(shown.columns)
    fig_w = col_width or max(8, 1.25 * ncol)
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(fig_w, 0.3 * (len(shown) + 1)))
    ax.axis("off")
    tbl = ax.table(cellText=shown.values, colLabels=list(shown.columns),
                   bbox=[0, 0, 1, 1], cellLoc="left", colLoc="left")
    tbl.auto_set_font_size(False)
    tbl.set_fontsize(8.5)
    for (r, c), cell in tbl.get_celld().items():
        cell.set_edgecolor(GRID)
        if r == 0:
            cell.set_facecolor("#f1f0ec")
            cell.set_text_props(weight="bold", color=INK)
        else:
            cell.set_text_props(color=INK2)
    tbl.auto_set_column_width(list(range(ncol)))
    ax.set_title(title, loc="left", fontsize=11, fontweight="bold", color=INK, pad=4)
    fig.savefig(path)
    plt.close(fig)


def fig_linear_vs_logistic(path):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
    fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4.2))
    # Linear: numeric y
    x = rng.uniform(0, 5, 60)
    y = 0.8 + 0.55 * x + rng.normal(0, 0.35, 60)
    b1, b0 = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)
    xs = np.linspace(0, 5, 100)
    axes[0].scatter(x, y, s=28, color=BLUE, edgecolor="white", linewidth=1)
    axes[0].plot(xs, b0 + b1 * xs, color=INK, lw=2)
    axes[0].set_title("Linear regression: predict a number")
    axes[0].set_xlabel("Rarity tier (example)")
    axes[0].set_ylabel("log10(price)")
    axes[0].text(0.1, 3.7, "ŷ = b₀ + b₁x", fontsize=12, color=INK)
    # Logistic: 0/1 y
    x2 = rng.uniform(0, 5, 80)
    p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(2.2 * (x2 - 2.6))))
    y2 = (rng.uniform(size=80) < p).astype(int)
    jit = rng.uniform(-0.03, 0.03, 80)
    axes[1].scatter(x2, y2 + jit, s=28, c=np.where(y2 == 1, ORANGE, BLUE),
                    edgecolor="white", linewidth=1)
    axes[1].plot(xs, 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(2.2 * (xs - 2.6)))), color=INK, lw=2)
    axes[1].axhline(0.5, color=INK2, lw=1, ls="--")
    axes[1].set_yticks([0, 0.5, 1])
    axes[1].set_title("Logistic regression: predict a probability")
    axes[1].set_xlabel("Rarity tier (example)")
    axes[1].set_ylabel("P(valuable)")
    axes[1].text(0.1, 0.83, "p = σ(b₀ + b₁x)", fontsize=12, color=INK)
    axes[1].text(4.15, 0.07, "0 = not valuable", fontsize=9, color=BLUE)
    axes[1].text(0.05, 0.9 - 0.18, "", fontsize=9)
    axes[1].text(3.85, 0.9, "1 = valuable", fontsize=9, color=ORANGE)
    fig.tight_layout()
    fig.savefig(path)
    plt.close(fig)


def fig_sigmoid(path):
    z = np.linspace(-7, 7, 300)
    s = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8.5, 4.2))
    ax.fill_between(z, 0, 1, where=z >= 0, color=ORANGE, alpha=0.08, lw=0)
    ax.fill_between(z, 0, 1, where=z < 0, color=BLUE, alpha=0.08, lw=0)
    ax.plot(z, s, color=INK, lw=2.2)
    ax.axhline(0.5, color=INK2, lw=1, ls="--")
    ax.axvline(0, color=INK2, lw=1, ls="--")
    ax.set_xlabel("Linear score  z = b₀ + b₁x₁ + … + bₖxₖ   (log-odds)")
    ax.set_ylabel("σ(z) = probability of class 1")
    ax.set_title("The sigmoid squeezes any score into a probability between 0 and 1")
    ax.text(-6.6, 0.88, "σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e^(−z))", fontsize=13, color=INK)
    ax.text(-6.6, 0.32, "z < 0  →  p < 0.5  →  predict 0\n(not valuable)",
            fontsize=10, color=BLUE)
    ax.text(2.0, 0.32, "z > 0  →  p > 0.5  →  predict 1\n(valuable)",
            fontsize=10, color=ORANGE)
    for zz in (-2, 2):
        ss = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-zz))
        ax.scatter([zz], [ss], s=50, color=INK, zorder=3, edgecolor="white", lw=1.5)
        ax.annotate(f"z = {zz} → p = {ss:.2f}", (zz, ss),
                    xytext=(zz + 0.35, ss - 0.08),
                    fontsize=9.5, color=INK2)
    ax.set_ylim(-0.02, 1.02)
    fig.savefig(path)
    plt.close(fig)

def fig_coefs(path, names, coefs, title, xlabel):

    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.5, 3.8))
    vals = np.array(coefs)[order]
    ax.barh(np.array(names)[order], vals,
            color=[BLUE if v >= 0 else ORANGE for v in vals], height=0.55)
    ax.axvline(0, color=INK2, lw=1)
    for i, v in enumerate(vals):
        ax.text(v + (0.004 if v >= 0 else -0.004) * max(abs(vals)) * 10, i, f"{v:+.3f}",
                va="center", ha="left" if v >= 0 else "right", fontsize=9, color=INK)
    ax.set_xlabel(xlabel)
    ax.set_title(title)
    pad = max(abs(vals)) * 0.25

def main():
    ap = argparse.ArgumentParser()
    ap.add_argument("--data-dir", default=".")
    ap.add_argument("--out", default=".")
    args = ap.parse_args()
    data_out = os.path.join(args.out, "data")
    img = os.path.join(args.out, "img")
    os.makedirs(data_out, exist_ok=True)
    os.makedirs(img, exist_ok=True)

    df, raw_rows = load_and_prepare(args.data_dir)
    df.to_csv(os.path.join(data_out, "pokemon_regression_data.csv"), index=False)

    # ---- Train/test split: ONE split, reused by every model ----
    train, test = train_test_split(df, test_size=0.20, random_state=SEED,
                                   stratify=df["Valuable"])
    assert set(train["ID"]).isdisjoint(set(test["ID"])), "train/test overlap!"
    train.to_csv(os.path.join(data_out, "train_set.csv"), index=False)
    test.to_csv(os.path.join(data_out, "test_set.csv"), index=False)

    X_train, X_test = train[FEATURES].values, test[FEATURES].values

    # ---- Linear regression: numeric target log10(price) ----
    lin = LinearRegression().fit(X_train, train["Log10_Price"])
    yl_pred = lin.predict(X_test)
    yl_true = test["Log10_Price"].values
    rmse = float(np.sqrt(mean_squared_error(yl_true, yl_pred)))
    mae = float(mean_absolute_error(yl_true, yl_pred))
    r2 = float(r2_score(yl_true, yl_pred))
    dollar_mae = float(np.median(np.abs(10 ** yl_true - 10 ** yl_pred)))
    baseline_rmse = float(np.sqrt(np.mean((yl_true - train["Log10_Price"].mean()) ** 2)))

    # ---- Logistic regression: two-class label Valuable ----
    logit = LogisticRegression(max_iter=10000).fit(X_train, train["Valuable"])
    p_test = logit.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]
    yc_pred = (p_test >= 0.5).astype(int)          # threshold 0.5 -> class
    yc_true = test["Valuable"].values
    cm = confusion_matrix(yc_true, yc_pred)

    # ---- Gaussian Naive Bayes on the same split, for comparison ----
    nb = GaussianNB().fit(X_train, train["Valuable"])
    nb_p = nb.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]
    nb_pred = nb.predict(X_test)
    nb_cm = confusion_matrix(yc_true, nb_pred)

    def cls_metrics(pred, prob):
        return {"accuracy": float(accuracy_score(yc_true, pred)),
                "precision": float(precision_score(yc_true, pred)),
                "recall": float(recall_score(yc_true, pred)),
                "f1": float(f1_score(yc_true, pred)),
                "roc_auc": float(roc_auc_score(yc_true, prob))}

    results = {
        "raw_rows": raw_rows, "n_rows": len(df), "n_train": len(train), "n_test": len(test),
        "valuable_share": float(df["Valuable"].mean()),
        "train_valuable": int(train["Valuable"].sum()), "test_valuable": int(test["Valuable"].sum()),
        "features": FEATURES,
        "linear": {"rmse": rmse, "mae": mae, "r2": r2, "median_abs_dollar_error": dollar_mae,
                   "baseline_rmse": baseline_rmse, "intercept": float(lin.intercept_),
                   "coef": dict(zip(FEATURES, map(float, lin.coef_)))},
        "logistic": {**cls_metrics(yc_pred, p_test), "confusion": cm.tolist(),
                     "intercept": float(logit.intercept_[0]),
                     "coef": dict(zip(FEATURES, map(float, logit.coef_[0]))),
                     "odds_ratio": dict(zip(FEATURES, map(float, np.exp(logit.coef_[0]))))},
        "naive_bayes": {**cls_metrics(nb_pred, nb_p), "confusion": nb_cm.tolist()},
        "majority_baseline_accuracy": float(max(yc_true.mean(), 1 - yc_true.mean())),
    }
    with open(os.path.join(args.out, "results.json"), "w") as f:
        json.dump(results, f, indent=2)

    # ---- Figures ----
    fig_linear_vs_logistic(os.path.join(img, "linear_vs_logistic.png"))
    fig_sigmoid(os.path.join(img, "sigmoid.png"))
    sample_cols = ["ID", "Name", "Era", "Rarity", "Market_Price"] + FEATURES + ["Log10_Price", "Valuable"]
    table_image(df[sample_cols].sample(8, random_state=1), os.path.join(img, "data_sample.png"),
                f"Prepared data sample ({len(df):,} rows × {len(sample_cols)} columns shown)")
    tcols = ["ID", "Name"] + FEATURES + ["Log10_Price", "Valuable"]
    table_image(train[tcols], os.path.join(img, "train_sample.png"),
                f"Training set — first 6 of {len(train):,} rows", max_rows=6)
    table_image(test[tcols], os.path.join(img, "test_sample.png"),
                f"Testing set — first 6 of {len(test):,} rows", max_rows=6)
    fig_split(os.path.join(img, "split.png"), len(df), len(train), len(test))
    fig_pred_vs_actual(os.path.join(img, "pred_vs_actual.png"), yl_true, yl_pred, r2, rmse)
    fig_residuals(os.path.join(img, "residuals.png"), yl_true, yl_pred)
    fig_coefs(os.path.join(img, "linear_coefs.png"), FEATURES, lin.coef_,
              "Linear regression coefficients", "Change in log10(price) per +1 unit of the feature")
    fig_coefs(os.path.join(img, "logistic_coefs.png"), FEATURES, logit.coef_[0],
              "Logistic regression coefficients", "Change in log-odds of 'valuable' per +1 unit")
    fig_confusion(os.path.join(img, "confusion_logistic.png"), cm,
                  "Logistic regression — confusion matrix")
    fig_confusion(os.path.join(img, "confusion_nb.png"), nb_cm,
                  "Naive Bayes — confusion matrix")
    fr, tr, _ = roc_curve(yc_true, p_test)
    fn, tn, _ = roc_curve(yc_true, nb_p)
    fig_roc(os.path.join(img, "roc.png"),
            [("Logistic", fr, tr, results["logistic"]["roc_auc"]),
             ("Naive Bayes", fn, tn, results["naive_bayes"]["roc_auc"])])
    fig_compare(os.path.join(img, "model_compare.png"), results)

    print(json.dumps(results, indent=2))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

On the 1,117 test cards, the model explains 75% of the variation in log price (R² = 0.750). The RMSE of 0.473 is half the 0.947 you would get by predicting the average price for every card. An MAE of 0.361 on the log scale means a typical prediction is off by a factor of about 2.3× (100.361). Because most cards are cheap, the median miss is only $0.18.

Because the target is log₁₀(price), a coefficient b means the price is multiplied by 10b for each one-unit increase in that feature, with the other features held the same.

  • Rarity_Tier (b = 0.432). Each step up the rarity scale multiplies the predicted price by 100.432 ≈ 2.70×. Going from a Common (tier 0) to a chase rare (tier 5) raises the predicted price by a factor of about 144, for an otherwise identical card. Rarity is by far the strongest driver of price.
  • Card_Age_Years (b = +0.056). Each extra year since release raises the predicted price by about 14% (100.056 ≈ 1.136). Older cards go out of print and get scarcer, so they cost more, even after accounting for rarity.
  • Retreat_Cost (b = −0.064) is a smaller effect. Each extra energy to retreat lowers the price by about 14%, holding the rest fixed. Bulky, slow cards are a bit less desirable. HP and Max_Damage have tiny per-point coefficients because they are measured in single points (+100 HP is only about ×1.30).

The model labels 92.1% of test cards correctly: (TN 928 + TP 101) / 1,117. That beats the 84.2% you would get by calling every card “not valuable.” When it says a card is valuable it is usually right (precision 0.89: only 13 false alarms). However, it misses 75 of the 176 valuable cards (recall 0.57). Those are mostly cards that are valuable for reasons the features don’t capture, the same chase cards the linear model underpredicts. If missing a valuable card is costly, lowering the threshold below 0.5 would trade some precision for higher recall.

What one coefficient means for the odds: Rarity_Tier (b = 1.71). Each step up the rarity scale adds 1.71 to the log-odds that a card is valuable, so it multiplies the odds by e1.71 ≈ 5.5, holding the other features fixed. For example, if a tier-3 card has odds of 1 to 9 of being worth $5+ (p = 0.10), the same card at tier 4 has odds of about 5.5 to 9 (p ≈ 0.38). Likewise, Card_Age_Years has an odds ratio of 1.21: each year of age raises the odds of being valuable by about 21%.

Logistic regression is the stronger model here. Naive Bayes finds slightly more of the valuable cards (112 vs 101), but it pays for that with 120 false alarms instead of 13. Its accuracy (0.835) is actually below the “always say not valuable” baseline. The likely reason is Naive Bayes’s assumption that features are independent within each class. Here they clearly are not: HP, Max_Damage, and Rarity_Tier rise together (V and ex cards are high-HP, hard-hitting, and rare). Naive Bayes counts that shared evidence several times and becomes overconfident. Logistic regression fits all the weights together, so correlated features share the credit. It also assumes no particular shape for each feature’s distribution, while Gaussian NB treats skewed counts like Retreat_Cost as bell curves.

eConclusions

Rarity is the price. Of everything printed on a Pokémon card, the rarity symbol tells you the most about what it is worth. Each step up the rarity ladder multiplies the expected price by roughly 2.7 and multiplies the odds of a card being worth $5+ by about 5.5. Gameplay stats such as HP, attack damage, and number of attacks barely matter once rarity is known. Collectors pay for scarcity and artwork, not for how strong a card is in a game.

Age adds value. Even with rarity held fixed, older cards cost more: about 14% more per year. This matches what earlier tabs showed for vintage vs modern cards. Sets go out of print, the supply of clean copies shrinks, and prices drift up.

What we can predict, and what we can’t. Using only card attributes, we can estimate a card’s price to within a factor of about 2.3 and explain 75% of the variation in log price. We can also flag cards worth pulling out of bulk with about 92.1% accuracy and high precision. That is practical for sorting a collection: if the model says a card is valuable, it almost always is. What these models cannot do is spot the true chase cards (a Charizard, a popular illustration rare), whose prices depend on which Pokémon it is and how much people like the art. Those cards are the ones the linear model underpredicts and the logistic model misses. Adding a feature for the Pokémon’s popularity, or its set, would be the natural next step.

Choosing a model. For the yes/no question “is this card worth $5?”, logistic regression beat Naive Bayes on accuracy, precision, F1, and AUC. This shows that when features are correlated, a model that learns their weights jointly is more reliable than one that assumes they are independent.
